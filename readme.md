# A Reading List for Self-Knowledge

The highest purpose of literature, or anything for that matter, is to point us toward the **true nature of the Self**.

Every human being inherits a mind conditioned by culture, memory, desire, fear and countless other psychological influences. The gradual dissolution of this conditioning—the emptying of one's cup—is the **MOST IMPORTANT** thing that is to be done. Everything else is secondary.

The books listed below have helped me in that direction. They come from diverse traditions -- Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism, Sufism, Christianity and even the secular — but they all share the same essential insight: the **psychological ego as the root of all human suffering**, and that freedom lies in transcending it.

I am sharing these books in the hope that they will guide others as they have guided me. Books alone won't do, you even risk becoming more proud of yourself having read them. Nothing can substitute lived experience and uncompromising honesty with oneself. But alongside these, books will be the best companions you can have on this journey.

---

## _Freedom from the Known_ — J. Krishnamurti

Krishnamurti is my favourite amongst modern spiritual teachers because his method (although he rejects all "methods") is primarily psychological and he rejects all authority, religious or otherwise. This cutting away of all mystical and religious fluff is why he appeals so much to my techncial mind, which seeks for psychological and scientific explanations rather than religious or teleological ones.

I have absorbed a lot from this short book, which is essentially a manifesto of his teachings.

---

## _The Prophet_ — Khalil Gibran

I first picked up this book when I was twelve or thirteen but left it incomplete. And I am glad that I did as I was far too young to appreciate its depth.

Gibran has the knack of expressing profound truths with astonishing simplicity and poetic beauty. An all time favourite, I return to it every now and then when a sudden event in my life reminds me of a line from one his poems.

---

## _The Book of Ten Masters_ — Puran Singh

The best book on the lives of the Ten Sikh Gurus.

Instead of presenting dry historical details, Puran Singh brings the lives of the Ten Sikh Gurus alive with unmatched poetic beauty and deep reverence. It is the book I would recommend to anyone seeking an introduction to Sikhism.

---

## _The Masnavi_ — Jalaluddin Rumi

For me, this is the greatest piece of literature ever written.

I have not even completed twenty percent of it, but am convinced of its unparalleled greatness. Every page dissolves the boundaries between poetry, philosophy, psychology, and mystical experience.

If there is one book written for the annihilation of the ego, this is it.

---

## _Jonathan Livingston Seagull_ — Richard Bach

A remarkably short story that hides extraordinary depth within.

Although Richard Bach was not an Advaitin, the journey of Jonathan is saturated with themes that resonate deeply with Advaita: freedom from limitation, transcendence of identity, and the discovery of one's true nature.

---

## _The Gospel in Brief_ — Leo Tolstoy

If your interest lies in understanding the teachings of Jesus rather than Christian theology, this is where I would recommend beginning.

Tolstoy distills the ethical and spiritual essence of the Gospels into a single narrative. Read with an open mind and the parallels between the teachings of Jesus and Advaita will strike you.

---

## _The Madman_ — Khalil Gibran

Another masterpiece from Gibran.

This is a collection of short 1 page parables, sometimes even just 2 lines. Gibran again showcases his genius by compressing insights into the human condition and spirutal truths into these mini-stories.

---

## _The Conference of the Birds_ — Fariduddin Attar

Another masterpiece of Sufi literature, this book predates and profoundly influenced Rumi.

It tells the allegorical story of a flock of birds searching for their king, the Simorgh. Beautifully written and rich with symbolism, it is among the crowning achievements of Sufi literature. Had The Masnavi not existed, this probably would have occupied the highest place.

---

## _Kabir Bijak_ — Kabir Sahib

If there is one poet-philosopher Indians ought to read more, it is Kabir.

He surpasses even Gibran in his ability to compress profound truth into a handful of words. 

One of the greatest treasures Indian civilization has produced

---

## _Old Path White Clouds_ — Thich Nhat Hanh

Perhaps the most accessible book on this list.

Presented as a novel, it narrates the life of the Buddha while naturally introducing the central principles of Buddhism. It succeeds remarkably well in combining biography with philosophy.

If you are beginning your exploration of Buddhism, this is the best starting point.

---

## _Siddhartha_ — Hermann Hesse

A deserved classic.

It follows the journey of a boy named Siddhartha (who is not Gautama Buddha) in his search for spiritual truth. I see glimpses of myself in the young Siddhartha.

---

## _Of Human Bondage_ — W. Somerset Maugham

Unlike other books on this list, this is not a spiritual or mystical work, but a novel.

I have included this because it offers one of the most honest and penetrating psychological examinations of attachment, desire, insecurity and the subtle ways in which the ego binds itself to suffering in an extremely simple yet beautiful way.
